\chapter{Evaluation}\label{chapter:evaluation}

\section{Evaluation Questions and Criteria}

This chapter evaluates RQ4: \emph{To what extent do the proposed reference
architecture and its implementation support the monitoring needs of
representative ROS~2 applications?}

The evaluation criteria are defined first, so that each experiment can be
related to the evidence it contributes:

\begin{description}
  \item[C1 --- Observation coverage.] The prototype should collect typed topic
  messages, service request/response events, and action feedback/status.

  \item[C2 --- Functional correctness and evidence.] Produced verdicts should
  match expected state changes or independently recorded outcomes and identify
  contributing records.

  \item[C3 --- Deployment variation.] The same interfaces should work in
  integrated and split deployments, including a heterogeneous multi-host
  placement.

  \item[C4 --- Automation and reuse.] A deployment specification should
  generate matching runtime YAML files, while experiments should reuse
  observation, representation, processing, transport, and evidence code.

  \item[C5 --- Architectural accommodation.] The reference architecture should
  be able to host the principal capabilities of ROSMonitoring, ROMoSu,
  digital-twin runtime verification, and the Rabiser et al.\ reference
  architecture through configuration and explicit extension points.
\end{description}

C1--C4 derive from the requirements in Chapter~\ref{chapter:design}; C5
derives from the research gaps in Chapter~\ref{chapter:related_work}. Resource
use and latency are reported as supporting observations, not as a performance
benchmark.

\section{Experiment Design}

The five experiments are designed to contribute complementary evidence.
E1 and E2 use deterministic stimulus nodes so every transition is known in
advance. E3 replaces these nodes with unmodified Nav2, a widely used ROS~2
navigation stack~\cite{macenski2020marathon}, in the Gazebo
simulator~\cite{koenig2004gazebo}. E4 adds two namespaces and a property using both
robots. E5 keeps the E4 checking graph but distributes the application,
monitor, and verifier across Ubuntu, Raspberry Pi, and macOS. The term
\emph{representative} therefore means that the set progresses from controlled
interface tests to existing navigation software, multi-robot correlation, and
heterogeneous placement. It does not claim to represent every robot or field
condition.

\section{User Workflow and Experiment Artifacts}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item identify ROS~2 sources and properties;

\section{E1: Topic Monitoring in an Integrated Deployment}

One workstation ran a stimulus node and the monitor as two ROS~2 processes. The
collector, converter, and verdict service were integrated in the monitor.
\texttt{/cmd\_vel} was published at approximately 5~Hz; \texttt{linear.x}
alternated between 1.85 and 0.2~m/s every three seconds. The property was
\[
  \texttt{linear.x} \leq 0.5\ \text{m/s}.
\]
A verdict was expected only when satisfaction changed. During 120 seconds,
593 records contained 40 expected changes. The monitor produced all 40
verdicts, no extra verdict, and attributed each verdict to the first record on
the new side of the threshold.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\caption{E1 results.}
\begin{tabular}{lr}
\toprule
Collected \texttt{/cmd\_vel} records & 593\\
Expected / produced verdicts & 40 / 40\\
Missed or additional verdicts & 0\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

E1 therefore establishes the complete integrated topic path for C1 and C2: the
collector, converter, and verdict service preserve the expected sequence of
property-state changes.

\section{E2: Topics, Services, and Actions}

E2 separates collection from checking while adding service and action
observations. All components still ran on one physical workstation, but in
separate processes: three deterministic stimulus processes, one monitor,
Mosquitto, and one verifier. The monitor collected observations through DDS,
wrote the records locally, and sent them over the local MQTT broker to the
verifier. During the 120-second reference run it collected:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \texttt{/cmd\_vel}, with the E1 speed pattern;
  \item \texttt{/reset\_pose} request and response events every three seconds;
  \item \texttt{/odom} at 5~Hz; and
  \item \texttt{/fibonacci} feedback and status for order-6 goals every five
  seconds.
\end{itemize}

Odd reset calls moved the pose near the origin; even calls deliberately
reported success without moving it. The reset-effect property required distance
to the origin to become at most 0.05~m within one second of a successful
response. This explains why a service response and a later topic record appear
in one verdict.

The speed property ran once inside the monitor and once in the verifier. This
duplication was intentional: it tested whether serialization and MQTT transport
preserved the result and record attribution, not whether two different
properties were needed.

E2 satisfies the remaining observation part of C1 and shows for C2/C3 that one
recorded stream retains its meaning across the local MQTT boundary.

\section{E3: One Nav2 Robot}

E3 used an unmodified TurtleBot3 Nav2 application in Gazebo. Gazebo, Nav2, the
mission process, monitor, broker, and verifier ran as separate processes on one
workstation. The monitor observed \texttt{/cmd\_vel}, rate-limited fields from
\texttt{/odom}, and feedback/status from \texttt{/navigate\_to\_pose}. It
checked a 0.2~m/s speed property and:
\[
  \text{goal status = succeeded} \ \land\  \text{duration} \leq 35\ \text{s}.
\]

The mission deliberately produced three outcomes, shown in
Figures~\ref{fig:e3-goals} and~\ref{fig:e3-goalc}. Goal A used an open lower
route. Before B, an obstacle closed that route, forcing the upper route.

\section{E4: Two Nav2 Robots}

E4 ran two namespaced TurtleBot3/Nav2 instances in Gazebo, deployed as shown
in Figure~\ref{fig:e4-deployment}. One monitor collected six topics: each
robot's \texttt{cmd\_vel}, \texttt{amcl\_pose}, and \texttt{odom}. Separate
speed properties required \(\texttt{linear.x}\leq0.3\)~m/s. A fleet property
required the distance between the two localized map-frame poses to remain at
least 1.0~m. Each new pose was paired with the latest pose of the other robot.

The code sizes in the public repository quantify this boundary. The reusable
\texttt{monitor/} package contains about 4{,}200 lines of Python in 23 modules
and was reused unchanged by every experiment. All property-specific plug-in
code across the case studies amounts to about 260 lines in four modules
(\texttt{CmdVelSpeedConverter}, \texttt{ThresholdVerdict}, the reset-effect
pair, and \texttt{RelativeSpeedConverter}). Each experiment therefore
implemented at most roughly one hundred lines of new property code while
reusing the collection, serialization, routing, transport, and evidence
implementation. The ten generated runtime YAML files and the unchanged
E4-to-E5 property code are further concrete outcomes of this separation: the
framework automates routine implementation work while leaving domain knowledge
visible.

\section{Accommodation of Existing Approaches}\label{sec:accommodation}

C5 asks whether the reference architecture can host the principal capabilities
of the reviewed approaches. For each of the four selected approaches, this
section maps its main components onto architecture roles and states how the
mapping is achieved. Three mapping statuses are used:

\begin{description}
  \item[Direct.] Achieved by configuring existing prototype components.
  \item[Adapter.] Achieved by developing a converter, verdict-service,
  transport, or exporter plug-in behind an existing stable interface.
  \item[Extension.] Supported by an architectural extension point that the
  prototype does not implement.
\end{description}

Figure~\ref{fig:compatibility-map} gives the overview; the tables below give
the role-level mappings. The purpose is to check responsibility coverage, not
to claim feature equivalence with the original systems.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \resizebox{\textwidth}{!}{\input{figures/compatibility_map}}
  \caption{High-level accommodation of four reviewed approaches. The

\subsection{Summary}

The monitoring data paths of all four approaches can be expressed with
configured components and plug-ins of the reference architecture. The
recurring boundaries are management and visualization planes, digital-twin
state, invasive instrumentation, and verdict-driven feedback, which map to
declared extension points rather than to implemented prototype features. This
satisfies C5 at reference-architecture level while marking exactly where
implementation work would remain.

\section{Answer to RQ4}

Within the evaluated scope, the answer is positive. C1 is supported by E1--E3,
which collect topics, service request/response events, and action
feedback/status; Table~\ref{tab:observable-matrix} shows that no reviewed
approach combines this application-level coverage. C2 is supported by expected stimulus transitions, independent
mission and Gazebo logs, replay, and verdict attribution. C3 is supported by
integrated, split same-host, and three-machine placements. C4 is supported by
ten generated runtime configurations and reuse of the same observation,
transport, and evidence code, including unchanged E4 property logic in E5 and
the measured ratio of about 4{,}200 reused framework lines to about 260
property-specific lines. C5 is supported by the role-level mappings in
Section~\ref{sec:accommodation}.

The claim is limited to ROS~2 Kilted, the selected interfaces and plug-ins, one
local network, up to two simulated robots, and runs lasting a few minutes.
